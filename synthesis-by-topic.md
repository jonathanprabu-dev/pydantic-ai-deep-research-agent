# US healthcare technology: research synthesis by topic

Findings from six research runs, regrouped by theme rather than by run.

- US healthcare data infrastructure: claims clearinghouses, EDI, prior authorization
- Unsolved technical problems in US healthcare IT infrastructure
- US healthcare interoperability vendors and funding landscape
- Documented failures and unsolved problems in healthcare AI
- LLMs and AI for medical terminology mapping and normalization
- FDA real-world performance monitoring and drift detection

**How to read this.** Every factual claim below is cited to a source one of the reports actually retrieved. Passages marked **[Inference]** are my reasoning across reports, not something any source states — those carry the weight of an argument, not a finding. Written by hand from the six reports rather than generated by the pipeline, because the daily model quota ran out; a reproducible version is one command away once quota resets.

---

## Overview

Across six runs the same shape keeps appearing: **the binding constraint in US healthcare technology is verification, not capability.** It takes two forms — verifying that data means what you think on the way in, and verifying that a deployed system still works over time. Neither is a modelling problem. Both are infrastructure, both are now the subject of dated federal mandates, and both are documented by regulators as unsolved.

The strongest evidence for this reading is that two runs with different queries hit the same wall from opposite directions, which is the closest thing to independent corroboration the research produced.

---

## 1. The regulatory clock is the forcing function

Everything downstream is shaped by one rule with hard dates.

**CMS issued the Interoperability and Prior Authorization Final Rule (CMS-0057-F) on 17 January 2024, applying to Medicare Advantage, Medicaid, CHIP and QHP issuers on the federally facilitated exchanges.**

It builds on the 2020 CMS Interoperability and Patient Access rule (CMS-9115-F), adding FHIR-based API requirements and new operational obligations for prior authorization.

- Evidence: [CMS Interoperability and Prior Authorization Final Rule (CMS-0057-F)](https://www.cms.gov/initiatives/burden-reduction/overview/interoperability/policies-regulations/cms-interoperability-prior-authorization-final-rule-cms-0057-f)

**Impacted payers must implement FHIR-based prior authorization APIs by 1 January 2027, decide standard requests within 7 calendar days and urgent ones within 72 hours, and CMS estimates at least $16 billion in savings over ten years.**

The API deadline moved from the original 1 January 2026 to 1 January 2027 after stakeholder feedback. Operational provisions — decision timeframes and denial-reason disclosure — take effect 1 January 2026, with the first public reporting of prior-authorization metrics due 31 March 2026, and MIPS electronic prior-authorization attestation beginning in the 2027 performance period.

- Evidence: [CMS Interoperability and Prior Authorization Final Rule (CMS-0057-F)](https://www.cms.gov/initiatives/burden-reduction/overview/interoperability/policies-regulations/cms-interoperability-prior-authorization-final-rule-cms-0057-f)

**[Inference]** A dated mandate on a large, identified set of payers creates forced buying on a schedule. That is unusual and it is the single most actionable fact in the whole body of research: whatever gets built here has a deadline-driven customer rather than a speculative one.

## 2. Transport is largely solved; meaning is not

This is the distinction that organises the technical findings.

**As of November 2025, eleven data exchanges hold QHIN status under TEFCA — more than double the five designated at go-live in December 2023 — supporting over 71,000 participating sites.**

Oracle Health was among the more recent designations. TEFCA's three-layer framework mandates FHIR R4 and a network-of-networks model in which a single IAL2 identity verification unlocks data across QHINs.

- Evidence: [Oracle Health designated QHIN under TEFCA data sharing](https://www.healthcaredive.com/news/oracle-health-qhin-designation-tefca/806217/)

**Inconsistent patient demographic data causes record-linkage failures during QHIN-to-QHIN exchange.**

When QHINs exchange queries, records may fail to link if demographic data is inconsistent — undermining the goal of seamless nationwide exchange even where transport succeeds.

- Evidence: [TEFCA Health Tech Implementation Challenges Guide](https://www.invene.com/blog/tefca)

**The absence of a national patient identification and matching strategy worsens privacy and safety risks.**

CHIME told CMS and ASTP/ONC, in response to their RFI on the health technology ecosystem, that without a nationwide identification and matching strategy these risks are significantly exacerbated. This remains a systemic barrier to trusted exchange.

- Evidence: [CHIME Responds to CMS & ASTP/ONC's RFI on the Health Technology Ecosystem](https://chimecentral.org/chime/resource-post/chime-responds-to-cms-astponc-rfi-on-health-technology-ecosystem)

**Before joining TEFCA, organisations must resolve patient identity problems, normalise clinical terminologies and deploy FHIR APIs.**

These are prerequisites, not consequences — each organisation carries the cost independently before the network returns anything.

- Evidence: [2026 Healthcare Interoperability Report](https://www.trovehealth.io/insights/?post=trusted-exchange-framework-statistics-2026)

**[Inference]** The pipes are laid and what flows through them is ambiguous. Because identity, terminology and API work are per-organisation preconditions repeated across thousands of entities, they have the economic shape of something productisable — the same work done many times over.

## 3. The EHR integration wall, found twice independently

**Many EHR systems lack user-friendly FHIR APIs and app integration, forcing providers into manual workarounds.**

Found via the RFI on the health technology ecosystem: providers fall back on external applications requiring manual data handling.

- Evidence: [Health Technology Ecosystem RFI response](https://fah.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/RFI-Health-Technology-Ecosystem-61225-FINAL.pdf)

**Lack of interoperability between AI systems and existing health IT infrastructure hinders integration.**

AI tools often cannot exchange data with electronic health records due to differing standards and proprietary interfaces, creating operational friction and limiting utility.

- Evidence: [Overcoming Barriers to Artificial Intelligence Adoption in Clinical Settings](https://www.preprints.org/manuscript/202603.0316)

**[Inference]** These two findings came from different runs with different queries — one mapping interoperability infrastructure, one cataloguing AI failure modes — and landed on the same obstacle from opposite sides. Nothing else in the six reports was corroborated this way. Note the second source is a preprint and not peer-reviewed; the convergence is what makes it credible, not the individual citation.

## 4. Administrative burden is quantified, and prior auth remains unfixed

**Prior authorization costs providers roughly $34,000 per year each, consuming an average of 13 hours per week.**

CMS's own estimate, framed as time that could otherwise go to patient care.

- Evidence: [Moving Prior Authorization into the 21st Century](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/blog/moving-prior-authorization-21st-century)

**Providers continue to experience prior-authorization delays that postpone diagnoses and interrupt treatment.**

The American Hospital Association reports these delays persist, pointing to lingering workflow and technical gaps despite decades of electronic data interchange.

- Evidence: [AHA Comments on CMS' Interoperability and Prior Authorization Proposed Rule](https://www.aha.org/lettercomment/2026-06-15-aha-comments-cms-interoperability-and-prior-authorization-proposed-rule)

**Many payers and providers report being unprepared for the interoperability and prior-authorization requirements.**

A 2025 WEDI survey identified the top three challenges as forming a cohesive enterprise strategy, digitising prior-authorization policies, and related implementation hurdles.

- Evidence: [Many payers, providers unprepared for interoperability and prior authorization, WEDI finds](https://www.healthcarefinancenews.com/news/many-payers-providers-unprepared-interoperability-and-prior-authorization-rule-wedi-finds)

**[Inference]** Decades of EDI investment did not eliminate the friction, which is evidence that the problem was never purely transport. Combined with §1, the position is: a mandate is coming, the incumbents' existing approach has not solved it, and the people who must comply say they are not ready.

## 5. The measurement void beneath clinical AI

The most striking finding of the six runs, because it is a regulator publicly documenting its own inability.

**On 30 September 2025 the FDA's Digital Health Center of Excellence issued a Request for Public Comment on measuring and evaluating the real-world performance of AI-enabled medical devices, including detecting and managing performance drift, with comments due 1 December 2025 to docket FDA-2025-N-4203.**

The RFI asks for approaches deployed at scale and supported by real-world evidence, covering metrics, monitoring triggers, data sources and human-AI interaction. It is explicitly not draft or final guidance.

- Evidence: [Evaluating AI-enabled Medical Device Performance in the Real-World](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/digital-health-center-excellence/request-public-comment-measuring-and-evaluating-artificial-intelligence-enabled-medical-device)

**Nearly half of FDA-approved AI/ML medical devices did not report a clinical study, and over half reported no performance metric at all.**

Baseline performance data is frequently missing, making it difficult to establish the reference points that drift detection requires.

- Evidence: [Evaluating transparency in AI/ML model characteristics](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-025-02052-9)

**FDA reporting data is inconsistent, with demographic and socioeconomic characteristics underreported.**

Without demographic detail, monitoring cannot detect drift that disproportionately affects specific subpopulations.

- Evidence: [A scoping review of reporting gaps in FDA-approved AI devices](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11450195/)

**AI models fail on deployment for reasons unrelated to model quality: contextual mismatch and biased training data.**

A Harvard-led study in *Nature Medicine* found systems fail to account for local workflow nuances, equipment variation and patient population differences, causing performance drops outside the original research environment. Separately, models trained on data reflecting historical inequities produce disparate diagnostic accuracy across populations.

- Evidence: [Harvard Study Highlights Contextual Errors and Clinical AI Limits](https://www.labmanager.com/harvard-researchers-warn-contextual-errors-may-limit-medical-ai-across-clinical-settings-34997)
- Evidence: [Artificial intelligence in healthcare delivery: prospects and challenges](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2949916X24000616)

**[Inference]** You cannot detect drift from a baseline nobody recorded. That makes the missing artifact arguably an evidence layer — standardised performance reporting — rather than a drift detector as such. This is my hypothesis, not a claim any source makes, and nothing in the research shows anyone would pay for it.

## 6. Who already occupies these gaps

**Verato leads the enterprise master person index market, and the EMPI sector is projected to grow from $1.54 billion in 2025 to $3.90 billion by 2034.**

Top rankings from Black Book and health-system surveys in 2026, with efficiency gains reported in large deployments.

- Evidence: [Verato named #1 vendor in Enterprise Patient Identity (EMPI)](https://verato.com/news/verato-has-been-named-the-1-vendor-in-enterprise-patient-identity-empi-patient-matching-for-revenue-cycle-management/)
- Evidence: [Health System Clients Rate Verato #1 in Enterprise Patient Identity](https://www.newswire.com/news/health-system-clients-rate-verato-1-in-enterprise-patient-identity-empi-and)

**Health Gorilla holds TEFCA QHIN designation from December 2023, dual QHIN/QHIO status, and raised a $50 million Series C.**

Positioned as a national exchange platform for AI-ready clinical data.

- Evidence: [Health Gorilla Secures $50 million in Series C Funding](https://www.healthgorilla.com/blog/health-gorilla-secures-50-million-in-series-c-funding)
- Evidence: [Webinar: Health Gorilla Achieved QHIN Designation](https://www.healthgorilla.com/home/resources/video-library/webinar-health-gorilla-achieved-qhin-designation-what-it-means-for-your-organization)

**Clinical terminology normalisation is a mature vendor market — IMO, Wolters Kluwer Health Language, Clinical Architecture and Rhapsody — projected to reach $2.5 billion by 2027 from $1.0 billion in 2022.**

IMO took a growth investment from Warburg Pincus in 2016 (a co-investment case study puts it at $10.0 million) and launched IMO Studio in 2023.

- Evidence: [IMO Announces Growth Investment from Warburg Pincus](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/intelligent-medical-objects-imo-announces-growth-investment-from-warburg-pincus-300341240.html)

**[Inference]** Identity matching and FHIR aggregation have named, funded incumbents. The funding picture is thin and stale — the clearest figure is nine years old — because Crunchbase and PitchBook are paywalled and the fetcher drops them. Absence of recent funding data here is a limitation of the method, not evidence of a quiet market.

## 7. Where an intuition met measurement and lost

Recorded because the correction is more useful than the original idea.

**Machine-learning methods reach up to 0.99 accuracy for LOINC and 0.95 for SNOMED CT mapping, while LLMs with retrieval-augmented generation achieve only 50–60% on exact medical code lookup — even when the correct code is present in the retrieval set.**

SNOMED CT contains over 350,000 active concepts, and LLMs struggle with fine-grained disambiguation at that scale.

- Evidence: [LLMs and RAG for Medical Vocabulary Validation](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/Delivery.cfm/2e17a595-0ad3-4bb1-8970-c8ff41b5108f-MECA.pdf?abstractid=6545515&mirid=1)

**Of 19 studies integrating SNOMED CT with LLMs, 89% reported performance improvements — but only 51% of 37 surveyed studies offered direct comparisons.**

Fewer than half ran head-to-head evaluations, weakening the evidence base for claimed accuracy gains.

- Evidence: [Use of SNOMED CT in Large Language Models](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11494256/)

**[Inference]** I proposed terminology normalisation as the opening: unsolved at the meaning layer, apparently unowned, and a natural fit for LLMs. Both halves were wrong. The vendors existed and an earlier run simply never asked about them; and the technology thesis was backwards — LLMs lose to specialised methods at precisely this task. "LLMs are good at semantics" was an intuition about the technology, not a measurement of the job.

## Contradictions and corrections

- **Terminology normalisation: corrected.** Run 3 found no commercial owner and I read that as an opening. Run 5, which named candidate vendors in the query, found a mature market with several established players. The first run was not wrong — it was never asked. **Absence of a finding is not evidence of absence.**
- **LLM suitability: reversed.** My hypothesis that LLMs suit terminology mapping is contradicted by measured accuracy — classical ML at 0.95–0.99 versus 50–60% for LLM+RAG.
- **Market share estimates vary.** Reports cite AI-accelerator share for a single vendor at roughly 80% in one place and a 70–90% range in another, depending on methodology and hardware scope.
- **"No conflicts found" recurred and should be discounted.** Several runs reported no contradictions between sources. In at least two cases this reflects thin source diversity — many citations from one agency, or from vendors describing their own market — rather than genuine corroboration.

## Open questions

These are things the material cannot answer, not topics wanting more detail.

- **What industry actually told the FDA.** The comments on docket FDA-2025-N-4203 are companies stating in writing what they can and cannot do about drift detection. This is the highest-value primary evidence available and none of the six runs retrieved it.
- **Whether health systems buy AI monitoring at all**, or treat it as the device vendor's responsibility. This determines whether performance monitoring is a business or a feature, and no search can answer it — it requires talking to someone who runs clinical AI at a provider.
- **Current funding reality.** Round sizes, investors and valuations sit behind paywalled databases. The best figure retrieved is from 2016.
- **Whether anyone already sells clinical AI drift monitoring.** One vendor domain appeared repeatedly in the FDA run, so the field is not empty, but no run asked who occupies it. Given the terminology correction above, this silence should not be read as an opening.
- **Whether the 50–60% LLM mapping figure holds.** It rests on a single SSRN paper that is not peer-reviewed. Directional, not settled.

## What I would do next

**[Inference — this section is entirely my reasoning.]**

The research maps terrain; it validates nothing. Six runs and roughly 110 citations establish that regulators are publicly documenting problems they cannot solve, against dated deadlines — CMS forcing FHIR APIs by January 2027, the FDA asking outsiders how to measure drift across more than 1,250 authorised devices. Admitted incapacity plus a compliance clock is a genuine setup.

But the terminology correction shows how fast a plausible thesis dies on contact with measurement, and it cost one 6-request run to find out. The cheapest next tests, in order:

1. **Read the FDA docket comments.** Public, primary, and directly about what the market cannot do.
2. **Ask one person who runs clinical AI at a health system:** when your sepsis model degrades, how do you find out? If the answer is "we don't," that outweighs everything above. If it is "our vendor handles it," monitoring collapses into a feature.
3. **Only then** consider building. Not before both.
